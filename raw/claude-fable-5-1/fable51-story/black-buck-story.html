Falklands War · 30 April – 1 May 1982 · Operation Black Buck 1

One bomber. Eleven tankers. 8,000 miles.

The RAF needed to bomb the runway at Port Stanley, but the nearest British airfield — Ascension Island — was 3,380 nautical miles away. A single Vulcan couldn't get there and back on its own fuel. So eleven Victor tankers flew with it in a relay, passing fuel down the line and turning for home one by one, until only the bomber was left.

1The problem

Stanley is 3,380 nautical miles from the nearest British runway

That's London to New York and then some — each way. A loaded Vulcan can't reach the Falklands and return on its own tanks, so the plan was a chain of Victor tankers: tankers refuelling the bomber, and tankers refuelling other tankers so they could stay with it longer.

Planned: 15 tanker sorties and 18 refuellings to put one bomber over the target.
22350Z · take-off

Thirteen aircraft leave Ascension in the dark

Two Vulcans and eleven Victors stream off the runway a minute apart. Almost at once the lead Vulcan's cabin won't pressurise and it turns back. The reserve — XM607, flown by Flt Lt Martin Withers — is now the attack aircraft. A tanker with a hose fault also aborts and a spare slots in.

Fuel on board the bomber: 74,000 lb — burning 16,000 lb an hour at this weight.
3T+1h45 · first hand-over

Four tankers fill four others, then go home

This is the relay. The first four Victors pump their spare fuel into the next four and turn back. A fifth, flown by Wg Cdr Colin Seymour, tops up the bomber twice and heads home too. Thirteen aircraft have become five: four tankers and the Vulcan.

Why tankers refuel tankers: a Victor can't carry enough to go all the way south and back, so the ones that go furthest are topped up by the ones that don't.
4T+4h · second hand-over

Two more tankers empty themselves into the last two

Halfway down the Atlantic, two Victors give almost everything they have to the pair that will go furthest — Bob Tuxford and Steve Biglands — and turn for Ascension. They'll land nearly dry. Three aircraft remain: Tuxford, Biglands and the bomber.

Headwinds were worse than forecast: every aircraft was burning more fuel than the plan allowed.
5T+5h · the storm

The refuelling probe breaks

In violent thunderstorms near 40°S, Tuxford refuels the bomber, then trails his hose for Biglands — who was meant to be the final tanker. The basket is whipping around. Biglands connects, and his probe snaps off. He can't take fuel now, so the roles swap: Biglands hands back what he can spare and turns home, and Tuxford carries on south instead.

Tuxford's problem: he was supposed to turn back here with 64,000 lb. Going further means he may not have the fuel to get home.
6T+6h25 · last fuel

Tuxford gives what he can and turns north — short of fuel

About 400 miles from Stanley, Tuxford's Victor makes the final transfer. It is 7,000 lb less than the bomber expected. He turns for Ascension nearly 3,000 miles away, knowing his aircraft will run dry hundreds of miles short unless someone comes to meet him — and he keeps radio silence so as not to give the raid away.

The bomber is now alone, with enough fuel for the attack and a rendezvous it has to find on the way home.
70738Z · the attack

Down to 300 ft, up to 10,000 ft, 21 bombs across the runway

Withers runs in low under Argentine radar, then climbs for the bomb run. Twenty-one 1,000 lb bombs are dropped in a line at an angle across the runway so that at least one must hit. One does, cratering the runway. The Vulcan banks hard left and heads north, signalling the code-word "Superfuse": success.

At the time this was the longest bombing raid ever flown.
8Morning · two rescues

Getting everyone home

Hearing "Superfuse", Tuxford finally breaks radio silence and asks for a tanker. Seymour — already back at Ascension from his first sortie — takes off again and finds him over a thousand miles out. Meanwhile the bomber, running late and low, is guided by a Nimrod to Barry Neal's Victor off the coast of Brazil, with under 8,000 lb of fuel left in its tanks.

Landings: Tuxford 1355Z after 14 h 05 min · Withers 1452Z after about 15 hours. Nobody was lost.

Who's who

The bomber

Flt Lt Martin Withers · Vulcan XM607

Took over when the lead Vulcan aborted, flew the attack and the 15-hour round trip. Distinguished Flying Cross.

The last tanker

Sqn Ldr Bob Tuxford · Victor XL189

Should have turned home before the storm; instead went on to give the bomber its last fuel, knowing he couldn't reach Ascension unaided. Air Force Cross.

The broken probe

Flt Lt Steve Biglands · Victor XH669

Started as a spare, became the planned final tanker, lost his probe in the storm and got home on what he had left.

Two sorties in one night

Wg Cdr Colin Seymour · Victor XL511

Refuelled the bomber twice early on, landed, then took off again to find and refuel Tuxford.

The way home

Sqn Ldr Barry Neal · Victor XH672

Waited off Brazil, was sent further south when the bomber ran late, and met it with a perfectly timed turn.

The abort

Sqn Ldr John Reeve · Vulcan XM598

Lead crew whose cabin seal failed on take-off. Flew the second raid, Black Buck 2, three nights later.
