Sources & reconstruction

Documented history

This follows Black Buck 1, the first raid, on 30 April–1 May 1982. The principal sources are first-person accounts by tanker captain Bob Tuxford, refuelling instructor Dick Russell and Vulcan pilot Martin Withers. They establish the cascading tanker plan, the broken probe, the reversal of tanker roles and the recovery rendezvous.

What this simulation reconstructs

Geography: Ascension and Stanley are geographically located; connecting tracks and rendezvous coordinates are representative. Aircraft, islands and lateral separations are enlarged. This is not a flight-track recording. The fuel-network view deliberately replaces geographic distance with readable spacing.

Time: chapter times interpolate the reported sequence over the RAF’s 15h 45m flight duration. They are approximate elapsed times, not a verified event log. The initial screen previews the first bracket; ↺ returns to take-off.

Fuel: the gauge is a relative, interpolated narrative model. It shows consumption and replenishment, not measured tank contents, reserve endurance or an engineering fuel-balance calculation. Animated dots indicate transfer direction, not a measured flow rate.

Fleet: the eleven outbound Victors are shown individually. V01–V09 are visual identifiers, not historical callsigns. XL189 identifies Tuxford’s Victor; WHITE 4 identifies Biglands’ role. Individual early pairings are schematic. Later recovery support is grouped into two representative tracks, one for the bomber and one for Tuxford; these do not depict every recovery sortie.

Why there is no “exact transfers” counter

Published summaries are inconsistent: RAF Stories uses 18 refuellings; the RAF’s 2022 article gives both 14 and 15 in different passages. The RAF’s concise history separately records 18 Victor sorties, which is a different measure. This reconstruction does not treat those numbers as interchangeable.

An air bridge to one bomber.

Avro Vulcan B.2

XM607 · Martin Withers
Ascension Island → Port Stanley

Ascension Island was the launch point for a mission across thousands of miles of open ocean. The attacking Vulcan could not carry enough fuel for the return journey. Neither could the tankers accompanying it.

The answer was a cascade. Victors passed fuel to other Victors, concentrating the available fuel into fewer aircraft as the formation travelled south. Each departing tanker needed enough to reach home itself. The last tanker would send the Vulcan towards the islands; a later wave would help it return.

On the night, equipment failures and a higher-than-expected fuel demand pushed the plan to its limits. XM607 reached Stanley, dropped its 21 bombs and returned safely. The refuelling crews made that flight possible.
