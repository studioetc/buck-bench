      How to read this

      Every tanker that turns back carries fuel the rest of the force can't use, so the Victors keep topping each other up. At each bracket, half the aircraft hand over fuel and go home. The ones that carry on are full again. When the Vulcan took its last fuel off the Falklands, that fuel had been through several tankers.

      Not to scale. The aircraft really flew close together. Here they're spread across "lanes", and the icons are enlarged, so you can follow each one. Refuelling close-ups are illustrative.

      Accuracy

      Documented facts include the launch times, the two aborts, the 34-minute gap with 9,200 lb burned, the storm at 40°S and the broken probe, the 7,000 lb shortfall, bombs at 07:38Z, "Superfuse" at 07:46Z, Neal's 210° turn, Tuxford's 14 h 05 min and the 14:52Z landing. The overall sequence follows Wikipedia's refuelling-plan diagram: seven Vulcan contacts outbound and one on the way back. Some crew accounts count six outbound contacts instead.

      Everything else is a reconstruction: positions and times between those fixes, which Red and White aircraft paired up, the Nimrod's launch time, the rendezvous position and the fuel figures. Treat them as approximate.

      RAF · OPERATION CORPORATE · 30 APRIL 1982

      Eleven tankers.
      One bomber.
      3,300 nautical miles each way.

      At 23:00 Zulu on 30 April, thirteen aircraft took off from Wideawake on Ascension Island. A Vulcan was going to bomb the runway at Port Stanley, but no Vulcan could fly that far unrefuelled, and neither could the Victor tankers feeding it. The Victors had to refuel each other as they went, in a shrinking cascade, until one tanker was left to give the bomber its final fill.

      This simulation replays that night on a map, from launch to landing, with refuelling close-ups, the thunderstorm that broke a tanker's probe, and the improvised recovery that brought everyone home.
